I'm happy to say that I completed my BSc in Computer Science from the University of London and graduated with First Class Honours.
What started out as a few hours of study after work eventually led into me quitting my job and pursuing the degree full-time.
After 4.5 years, it feels like a bittersweet farewell. At times, the challenges seemed insurmountable but overcoming them has given me a new perspective on the nature of learning. I've outlined my thoughts in the lengthy blog post linked below and I'd like to go over some of them here as well.

- College didn't drip-feed me information, it mostly served as a framework for learning. Things like grades, deadlines, peers sharing experiences, teacher feedback, all help to keep you on track. You still need to do your own research and the UK system heavily encourages this.

- Being self-taught becomes a challenge mid-career. I found myself heavily biased towards keeping up with only whatever tech stack I was using at work. Exploratory learning becomes a prohibitively expensive when all you have are a few hours after work and on the weekends.

- Knowledge is transferable in ways that will often surprise you. Thinking short-term, "when am I going to use this?", is a trap. You'll often find ways to leverage programming patterns and architecture across languages and frameworks. I used my React experience to build a music player in C++ using the JUCE framework, both make use of a hierarchical composition model.

- Team assignments are probably more useful after you gain some real-world experience. They're an easy way to experiment with roles you don't usually have access to at work. During my studies, I was a pretend designer, team lead, scrum master, and product manager. It's also a good chance to reflect on your own bad habits at work (procrastination, slipping deadlines, lack of involvement) by seeing them in others, and to apply some of the practices people use to deal with these situations.

Lastly, as I move on to resuming my career, I'm aware that some of the knowledge gained will start to fade away from lack of use, as it always does. But I’m confident that the real value of these years lies elsewhere: in the discipline it took to get to graduation, in learning how to push past the comfortable surface of a topic, and in proving to myself that I can still take on unfamiliar subjects and become proficient in them.
